# Research Memo – August 18, 2026

---

## Objectives

Following the discussion at 8/17, today's analysis focused on two questions:

1. How similar are the high-risk children identified by the Ahn and Amangku prediction models?
2. If the two models identify different children, what explains the differences?

---

# 1. Starting Point

Previous model evaluation showed that the current Main-Caregiver(MC) model performs better than the Amangku model, particularly in identifying high-risk children.

| Metric | MC Model | Amangku Model |
|---|---:|---:|
| MSE | **0.0454** | 0.0512 |
| Top 10% capture | **53.35%** | 46.19% |
| Top 10% precision | **33.5%** | 29.0% |

The Top 10% capture result means that the MC model identifies 53.35% of all eventual NDIS participants by targeting only the 10% of children with the highest predicted risk.

This raised the next question:

> Is the MC model simply ranking approximately the same children slightly better, or is it identifying a meaningfully different high-risk population?

---

# 2. Overall Agreement Between Predictions

Predictions were merged by `spine_id`, with no missing Amangku prediction among children in the MC prediction sample.

The overall correlations between the two predictions are:

| Measure | Correlation |
|---|---:|
| Pearson correlation | 0.7992 |
| Rank correlation | 0.6468 |

The Pearson correlation indicates substantial agreement in predicted risk levels overall.

However, the lower rank correlation suggests that the ordering of children by predicted risk differs meaningfully between the two models.

Because the main application of the model is risk ranking and targeting, this motivated a direct comparison of the high-risk lists.

---

# 3. Top 10% High-Risk List Overlap

For each model, children were ranked from highest to lowest predicted NDIS risk, and the Top 10% of each ranking was selected.

Each model selects approximately 376,185 children.

The two lists can be divided into three groups:

| Group | N | Description |
|---|---:|---|
| Both | 237,762 | Selected in the Top 10% by both models |
| MC Only | 138,423 | Selected only by the MC model |
| Amangku Only | 138,423 | Selected only by the Amangku model |

The overlap rate is:

**63.2%**

Therefore, about 63% of the MC Top 10% list is also selected by Amangku, while approximately 37% of the list differs.

This confirms that the improvement in prediction performance is not simply coming from a nearly identical ranking.

---

# 4. Are the Children Selected Differently Actually Different in NDIS Risk?

The next question was whether the children uniquely selected by one model are actually more likely to enter NDIS.

| Group | N | Actual NDIS rate |
|---|---:|---:|
| Both | 237,762 | **40.34%** |
| MC Only | 138,423 | **21.71%** |
| Amangku Only | 138,423 | **9.49%** |

This is one of the main results from today's analysis.

Children selected by both models have the highest realized NDIS rate, which is consistent with both models agreeing that these children are very high risk.

More importantly, among the disagreement cases:

> **MC-only children are more than twice as likely to eventually enter NDIS as Amangku-only children (21.7% vs. 9.5%).**

The NDIS rate among MC-only children is approximately 2.3 times the rate among Amangku-only children.

This directly explains an important part of the improvement in Top 10% targeting performance: the MC model replaces lower-risk children from the Amangku list with children who have substantially higher realized NDIS risk.

The group composition also reproduces the overall precision results:

- MC Top 10% precision: approximately **33.48%**
- Amangku Top 10% precision: approximately **28.99%**

---

# 5. Is the Disagreement Just Due to Children Near the Top 10% Cutoff?

A possible explanation is that the two models mostly agree, with disagreement occurring only for children located just above or below the Top 10% threshold.

To test this, I examined where children uniquely selected by one model are ranked by the other model.

| Group | Ranking under other model | Mean percentile | Median | Q25 | Q75 |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| MC Only | Amangku ranking | 30.12% | 23.31% | 14.56% | 40.46% |
| Amangku Only | MC ranking | 27.10% | 22.42% | 14.51% | 35.84% |

A lower percentile indicates a higher risk ranking.

The disagreement is therefore not concentrated immediately around the 10% cutoff.

For example, the median MC-only child is ranked only around the Top 23% by Amangku, and one quarter of MC-only children are ranked around the Top 40% or lower by Amangku.

Thus:

> The MC model is identifying a group of children that Amangku often ranks substantially lower, rather than merely moving children from the 11th percentile into the Top 10%.

This is especially notable because the realized NDIS rate among these MC-only children is 21.7%.

---

# 6. What Characteristics Differ Between the Groups?

The next step was to investigate why the models disagree.

## 6.1 Age and AEDC availability

| Characteristic | MC Only | Amangku Only | Both |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Mean age at rollout | **4.07** | 8.07 | 7.12 |
| AEDC observed | 29.67% | 30.22% | 22.95% |

The most immediate difference is age.

MC-only children are substantially younger than Amangku-only children.

AEDC availability, however, is almost identical between the two disagreement groups (29.7% vs. 30.2%), suggesting that simple AEDC availability does not explain the difference between the lists.

This does **not** yet establish whether the actual AEDC predictors are important.

---

## 6.2 Gender composition

The MADIP `CORE_GENDER` coding was confirmed as:

- `1` = male
- `2` = female
- `3` = unknown

The Top 10% disagreement groups differ substantially by gender:

| Group | Male | Female | Unknown |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| MC Only | **78.4%** | 21.5% | 0.1% |
| Amangku Only | 49.0% | **50.5%** | 0.5% |
| Both | 70.4% | 29.4% | 0.3% |

The MC model therefore places substantially more boys into the high-risk group.

This result is potentially relevant to the proposed addition of child-gender interaction terms to the model.

# 8. Adjusted Comparison Controlling for Age and Gender

To formally check whether the Ahn-only difference persists after accounting for age and gender, I estimated:

`NDIS ~ MC Only + age fixed effects + gender fixed effects`

using only disagreement cases (`Ahn Only` and `Amangku Only`).

The coefficient on `MC Only` was:

| Variable | Log-odds coefficient | Odds ratio | p-value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Ahn Only | 0.8888 | **2.43** | < 2e-16 |

Therefore:

> After controlling for age and gender, the odds of entering NDIS are approximately **2.43 times higher** among children selected only by the MC model than among children selected only by the Amangku model.

This is a descriptive adjusted comparison and should not be interpreted causally.

It does, however, confirm that the difference in realized NDIS risk is not explained simply by the age and gender composition of the two groups.

---

# 9. Current Interpretation

The analysis so far suggests the following sequence:

1. **The MC model performs better at Top 10% targeting.**
   - Capture: 53.35% vs. 46.19%
   - Precision: 33.5% vs. 29.0%

2. **The improvement is associated with a meaningfully different high-risk list.**
   - Only 63.2% of the Top 10% lists overlap.

3. **The children uniquely selected by MC are substantially more likely to enter NDIS.**
   - Ahn Only: 21.7%
   - Amangku Only: 9.5%

4. **The disagreement is not merely caused by observations close to the Top 10% cutoff.**
   - MC-only children are ranked around Top 30% by Amangku on average.
   - Amangku-only children are ranked around Top 27% by MC on average.

5. **MC-only children have a distinctive composition.**
   - younger
   - more likely to be male
   - lower household income
   - more likely to have parent DOMINO history

6. **Age composition alone does not explain the improvement.**
   - MC-only NDIS rates remain higher within individual age groups.

7. **Gender composition also does not fully explain the difference.**
   - After controlling for both age and gender, Ahn-only children still have approximately 2.43 times the odds of entering NDIS.

The remaining question is therefore:

> **What information in the MC model allows it to identify these high-risk children that Amangku ranks substantially lower?**
